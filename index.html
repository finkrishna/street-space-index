Street Space Index · v0.1 · an open, checkable estimate

Who gets the street?

Every street is a fixed amount of width, shared between people walking, riding buses, two-wheelers and cars. This project measures how much of that width each group gets, compared with how many people each group actually moves.

This is not definitive. It's an invitation to analysts to approach a shared problem systematically: how should dense cities allocate a scarce common good, street space, among the people who use it? Every number can be checked, challenged and replaced. Adjust the assumptions, test your own road, and send better data.

What the ITDP street audit adds

In 2022 ITDP India and Pune Municipal Corporation audited 11 Pune streets wider than 24 m (about 30 km), interviewing 2,300 people at peak hours. Three findings feed this model:

FOOTPATHS

70% have them, 30% use them

Most audited streets had footpaths, but only 30% of pedestrians walked on them. Encroachment and obstructions push people onto the road. Use the footpath usable slider to test this.

CORRIDOR USERS

32% walk, 40% public transport

On these corridors, people surveyed walked and rode buses far more than the city average. Try the ITDP corridor preset above. Respondents to a street survey probably over-represent walkers.

RANKING

JM Road best; Satara 3rd

The audit scores design quality, and this index scores width share. They agree on JM Road. On Satara Road they diverge: well-built, but walkers get little width.

Per-street widths are in the full ITDP report and will replace the assumed Karve and Satara cross-sections in the next version.

How it works

STEP 1

Measure the street

Split the kerb-to-kerb width into footpath, cycle track, bus lane, traffic lanes, parking and medians.

STEP 2

Count the people

Use the city's trip mode share: how many trips are made on foot, by bus, by two-wheeler, by car.

STEP 3

Divide the lanes

Shared lanes are split by how much road each vehicle type takes up (vehicles × PCU), not by headcount.

STEP 4

Compare

Index = width share ÷ people share. Above 1 means more street than riders; below 1 means squeezed.

lane demand(mode) = trips(mode) ÷ occupancy(mode) × PCU(mode)
space(mode) = [dedicated width + lanes × lane-demand share + parking share] ÷ total width
index(mode) = space(mode) ÷ trips(mode)

What could make this wrong

We lead with the ways this estimate could mislead, before anyone relies on it.

  • Registered isn't on the road. RTO totals rarely remove scrapped vehicles. Only about 38% of Pune's 26 lakh pre-2019 vehicles have fitted the now-mandatory high-security plates, which suggests a lot of old registrations are no longer driven.
  • City averages aren't corridor reality. A shopping street has many more walkers than the city average. A highway-like road has far fewer. Counts for the specific corridor would beat this.
  • Drawn width isn't usable width. Hawkers, poles and transformers take footpath space, so walkers are usually worse off than shown here.
  • Space given isn't space used. An under-used bus lane scores well on this index and still fails the city.
  • PCU is a simplification. At signals, two-wheelers fill gaps between cars, so they probably take less lane space than we assume.
