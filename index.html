This is not definitive. It's an invitation to analysts to approach a shared problem systematically: how should dense cities allocate a scarce common good, street space, among the people who use it? Every number can be checked, challenged and replaced. Move the assumptions, test your own road, and send better data.

Built on ITDP India and PMC's street audit

The Pune half of this project only works because of Pune's Walk & Cycle Analysis, by the Institute for Transportation and Development Policy (ITDP) India with Pune Municipal Corporation, released on Pune's Pedestrians' Day in December 2022. It's the most careful public look at how Pune's main streets actually work that we know of.

WHAT THEY MEASURED

11 streets, ~30 km

Every street wider than 24 m in the sample, cut into 0.5–1 km segments, both sides surveyed separately, morning and evening peaks, Oct–Nov 2022.

THREE LENSES

Design, observation, perception

Design against Pune's street guidelines, on-site counts of how people actually move, and 2,300 interviews, including caregivers, the elderly and people with disabilities.

WHAT WE TOOK

Sections, counts, usability

Each street's measured cross-section, peak counts of every mode, and the observed share of each stretch with a usable footpath and cycle track.

Why it exists. Pune has been unusually deliberate about streets: a 2008 mobility plan that set a 2031 goal of 90% of trips by walking, cycling and public transport; Urban Street Design Guidelines; a Pedestrian Policy; a 2017 Bicycle Plan; and, according to ITDP, close to half of the transport budget going to sustainable transport since 2016. The city proposed this assessment in 2022 to check whether that spending was working. Most cities never measure their own streets this way.

What it found, in its own words, summarised: 30% of surveyed length had footpaths that were present but unusable; on poorly performing streets up to 80% of people walked off the footpath; only 14% of 675 caregivers felt safe letting children walk to school alone; 34% would take up cycling if it were safe. Streets built to the city's own design guidelines did better in every survey. JM Road scored 25.5/30. Magarpatta–Mundhwa scored 7.

How it works

STEP 1

Measure the street

Split kerb-to-kerb width into footpath, cycle track, bus lane, traffic lanes, parking and other. Discount footpath and cycle track by the share actually usable.

STEP 2

Count the people

Peak counts where measured (Pune, Copenhagen); city mode share otherwise. Vehicles × occupancy = people.

STEP 3

Divide the lanes

Shared lanes split by how much road each vehicle type takes up (vehicles × PCU). Goods vehicles take space but carry no counted people.

STEP 4

Compare

Headline: share of street for walking, cycling and buses. Fairness: space per person in a motor vehicle ÷ space per person walking, cycling or on a bus.

people(mode) = count(mode) × occupancy(mode)
lane share(mode) = count × PCU ÷ Σ(count × PCU)
space(mode) = [dedicated width × usable + lanes × lane share + parking share] ÷ total width
headline = space(walk + cycle + bus/metro)  ·  ratio = [space ÷ people]motor ÷ [space ÷ people]walk, cycle, bus

What could make this wrong

  • Counts are taken at one point. Walking trips are short, so fewer walkers pass any given point than vehicles on long trips. A count measures flow through the street, not everyone who uses it.
  • Today's counts hide suppressed demand. Few people use Pune's cycle tracks partly because they're blocked. ITDP found 34% would cycle if it were safe. Empty tracks score as over-served here, which says more about the tracks than about demand.
  • Bus occupancy drives the bus numbers. One bus at 40 people outweighs dozens of cars. Move the slider to test it.
  • World streets are illustrative. Only Nørrebrogade has published counts. The others use estimated widths and city-level travel patterns, to give a sense of scale, not a measurement.
  • One section per street. ITDP drew one representative cross-section per street. Real widths vary block by block.
  • Registered isn't on the road. Pune RTO totals rarely remove scrapped vehicles. That affects the city context figures, not the street results.
